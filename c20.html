Chapter Twenty: The Watchman at the Gate

Stage Five — Healing: How is the heart healed?

My friend,

Now we go deeper, and I want to describe the skill this chapter teaches before I name it, because the name has unfortunate associations.

Here is the problem it solves. You have resolved, sincerely, not to be sharp with a particular person. And then one afternoon you are sharp with them, and you did not decide to be. It simply happened, and by the time you noticed, it had already happened.

Where did that come from?

It came from a chain of small interior movements you were not watching. The irritation that arrived at nine in the morning. The story you told yourself about their motives at eleven. The slight tightening you carried through lunch. By the time it reached your mouth, it had been building for hours and required no decision at all.

The desert tradition calls the remedy nepsis — watchfulness.

What It Is Not

I need to be precise, because this discipline goes wrong in a specific way.

Watchfulness is not anxious self-monitoring. It is not a fearful obsession with your own faults, not a running interior commentary in which you audit every thought for defects. That produces scrupulosity, which is a genuine affliction, and I have watched it wreck sincere people who thought they were being rigorous.

Watchfulness is sustained, loving attention to the movements of your heart. The tone is that of a physician observing a patient he cares about — interested, unhurried, entirely without panic. If your inner life has become a courtroom, you have taken a wrong turn. Go back and start again more gently.

The Prayer of the Watchman

Set a watch, Yahweh, before my mouth. Keep the door of my lips. — Psalm 141:3

Notice he asks God to do it. He does not undertake to guard himself by force of will, which is the approach most of us try first and which fails reliably.

And note that the tradition reads this prayer as pointing further in than the lips. The mouth only voices what the heart has already produced. If the watchman is asleep at the interior gate, the city is lost well before anything is said aloud.

Four Travelers at the Gate

Watch for four arrivals:

Thoughts — the ideas, memories, and mental images that knock at the mind.

Desires — the underlying wants pulling you toward an object or outcome.

Emotions — the interior weather. Sudden anger, heaviness, the particular warmth of gratified pride.

Impulses — the swift urge to act or speak before anything has been considered.

Three practices with them:

Catch the thought at the door. Notice a harmful thought as it arrives. You need not fight it. Do not invite it in and sit it down at your table — acknowledge it and let it go past. Most destructive thoughts do their damage during the hospitality, not the arrival.

Probe the hidden motive. When a desire arises, ask where its roots run. Is this a genuine reach for something good, or a disguised reach for status, control, or reassurance?

Recognize the recurring pattern. Pay attention to the impulses that repeatedly lead you off the road. Once you know the path into the thicket, you can choose differently before your feet start moving.

Imitation Virtue

Now a warning I wish someone had given me thirty years earlier.

The heart is remarkably skilled at imitation virtue — performing genuinely good acts for reasons that are quietly rotten. Charity that feeds pride. Visible discipline undertaken to feel superior. Service that is really a bid for a reputation.

The diagnostic question, when an impulse to act arises: what is this movement of my heart actually trying to produce?

If the honest answer is a sense of superiority, or the maintenance of an image, or a coldness toward the people you are ostensibly serving, then it is not of the Way regardless of how spiritual it looks from outside.

And here is the practical wisdom, which took me a long time to accept: usually you should still do the good thing. Mixed motives are the ordinary human condition, and a man who waits for pure motives before acting will never act at all. Do the good thing, and notice what it was mixed with, and let the noticing do its slow work. Purification comes through the doing, not before it.

The Witness

Solomon set the requirement plainly:

Keep your heart with all diligence, for out of it is the wellspring of life. — Proverbs 4:23

And Christ gave the same diagnosis, locating the trouble exactly where the desert tradition would later go looking:

For from within, out of the hearts of men, proceed evil thoughts... All these evil things come from within, and defile the man. — Mark 7:21, 23

From within. Not from your circumstances. Not from what other people do to you. This is unwelcome news and it is also the most hopeful sentence in the chapter — because circumstances are largely beyond your reach, and the interior is precisely where grace has access.

The Practice: Watchfulness

The one-thought trace. Once daily, take one moment when you spoke or acted badly and trace it backward. When did it start? What was the first movement? You will usually find it began hours earlier than you assumed.

The pause at the gate. When you notice a thought arriving that you know leads somewhere bad, do not argue with it. Simply do not take it up. Turn your attention elsewhere — a task, a prayer, the room you are in.

The motive question. Once a day, before some good thing you are about to do, ask what it is for. Then do it anyway.

Ask yourself this week: What thought have I been hosting rather than merely receiving? Where does my virtue have an audience in mind?

A Blessing

May you keep watch at the gate of your heart without fear.

May you see what is there without flinching and without panic. And may the watchman you become be gentle — first with others, and then, harder, with yourself.
