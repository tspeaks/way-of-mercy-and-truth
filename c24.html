Stage Six — Transformation: How does the heart change?

My friend,

Now a corrective, and it is aimed at a particular danger that arrives at exactly this point on the road.

By now you have a framework. Three pillars, seven stages, a diagnostic method, a set of practices. You understand a great deal more than you did. And the danger of understanding a great deal is that it hardens.

I know this because I hardened. Somewhere around my fifteenth year I noticed that my faith had become a system — comprehensive, defensible, and closed. I could account for everything. I had an answer for every objection. And something had gone quietly dead in me that I did not notice for a long while, because a closed system does not feel like a loss. It feels like mastery.

What I had lost was wonder, and Ephrem the Syrian is the physician for that particular illness.

The Teacher of Symbols

Ephrem does not teach through definitions. He teaches through poetry, symbol, and paradox — the infinite contained in the finite, the Boundless God within the narrow walls of a human womb.

His conviction: truth is an ocean and our minds are small vessels. He is not anti- intellectual, and this is not an excuse for lazy thinking. He is making a claim about scale. Real things exceed our capacity to hold them, and pretending otherwise does not make us more accurate — it makes us smaller.

Mystery, in this tradition, does not replace truth. It deepens it. A mystery is not a puzzle awaiting solution; it is the depth of the truth itself. The purpose of symbolic

language is not confusion but reverence — the cultivation of humility, wonder, and receptivity before things larger than the mind can contain.

The Discipline of Unknowing

We have touched this before. Here it comes into its own, and it needs guardrails, because it is easily counterfeited.

It is not skepticism. We do not deny that truth exists. We confess that we do not possess it exhaustively.

It is not relativism. Not all paths are equal. Mercy remains better than cruelty. Integrity remains better than deceit. Unknowing does not blur those lines and anyone using it to do so has misunderstood it, probably deliberately.

It is not indecision. You can act with complete steadfastness while knowing your understanding is partial. Most good action in the world is taken on partial understanding; the alternative is paralysis dressed as rigour.

It is not passivity. It is active receptivity, not a shrug.

The formula worth keeping: humility protects the heart from pride; unknowing protects the mind from arrogance.

They are different guards for different rooms, and a person can be genuinely humble in character and still intellectually insufferable. I have been exactly that, and it did real damage to people who deserved better.

The Pillars Refined

Truth stops being a collection of facts you own and becomes an alignment you pursue. You seek it diligently while knowing your view is never the whole.

Humility becomes the settled recognition that you are not the center — and that you have not captured God inside your systems of thought, however good the systems are.

Mercy softens and widens. This is the practical payoff, and it is considerable. When unknowing reminds you that you cannot see the whole of your neighbor's heart — their hidden pain, their history, what they were carrying this morning — patience becomes possible where it was not. Nearly all harshness rests on a certainty about someone that we do not actually possess.

The Witness

When I consider your heavens, the work of your fingers, the moon and the stars, which you have ordained; what is man, that you think of him? What is the son of man, that you care for him?

Psalm 8:3–4

David looks up and does not produce a treatise. He produces a question, and leaves it standing.

Test this. Paul spends eleven chapters of Romans working through the deepest matter he knows, and does not conclude with a summary of his findings. He concludes like this: Oh the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and the knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past tracing out! (Romans 11:33). The most rigorous thinker in the New Testament ends in wonder rather than mastery, and does so on purpose.

The Practice: Wonder as a Discipline

Fifteen minutes of unanalysed looking. Choose one small thing in creation — a leaf, light moving on a wall, the lines of a loved one's hand. Do not analyse or define it. Look until you feel the weight of its mystery. Then give thanks for a reality larger than your thoughts.

Hold one certainty loosely. Identify a conviction you hold with total confidence, that is not a matter of first importance. Sit for ten minutes with the possibility that your understanding of it is incomplete. Do not abandon it. Just loosen the grip.

Ask a question you cannot answer. And do not resolve it. Let it stay open for a week and notice what that feels like.

Ask yourself this week: Where has my faith become a closed system? What have I stopped finding astonishing that ought to astonish me?

A Blessing

May your heart become less like a closed fist and more like an open hand.

May you find peace in the recognition of your own limits, and may your wonder carry you toward mercy rather than away from it.
