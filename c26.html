Stage Six — Transformation: How does the heart change?

My friend,

We come to the deepest question in this stage, and the most hopeful thing in this book.

The Macarian Homilies — attributed to Macarius the Egyptian, though almost certainly by another hand, and none the worse for it — are not concerned with outward behavior or the mechanics of discipline. They go to the frontier: what is actually happening inside a human being, and what God is actually doing there.

The Divided Heart

The first thing they teach is that the heart is far larger and stranger than it appears — not merely the seat of passing feeling but the center of the entire person. And in our condition it is frequently a place of profound fragmentation.

The diagnosis is stark. The heart can become an interior wilderness, a darkened place where destructive movements build roads and highways to wander through the mind. During the struggle it is as though two opposing realities have sprung up inside the same person — light contending with darkness, rest warring with affliction.

You have felt this. A sincere desire to walk in truth and mercy, warring in the same hour against a surge of resentment or pride or despair that seems to come from nowhere and to belong to someone else.

Do not be terrified by this division. It is not evidence that your faith is fraudulent. It is the normal condition of a person in the middle of being remade, and the homilies are emphatic: even when the soul is tossed by the heaving of the passions and beaten by the winter storm of internal distress, the true Navigator desires to board the vessel and calm the sea.

The goal is a heart that moves from divided and darkened to whole, unified, and lit.

The Wood and the Iron

How does a heart this heavy ever rise? It cannot lift itself; that is the whole difficulty.

The homilies reach for an image from the prophets. Elisha's companion loses a borrowed axe head in the river, and Elisha throws in a stick, and the iron floats.

He cut down a stick, threw it in there, and made the iron float.

2 Kings 6:6

Iron does not float. It is heavy by nature — not by accident, not through some correctable error, but by what it is.

And the argument runs: if a piece of wood could raise iron against its own nature, how much more will God send His own Spirit to raise a soul that has sunk into the heavy water?

This is the promise, and it is larger than the one most of us are working with. You are not being asked to behave better. You are being offered a change of nature. Not merely commanded to perform good deeds — invited to share in the life of God Himself, who is willing to mingle His own life with our interior world.

The Iron Tool

Which raises the question that has been under this whole book: if God does the changing, what is my part?

The homilies refuse both easy answers.

Those who believe they can abolish their inner disorder by independent effort are, in the text's blunt phrase, vainly puffed up. Self-justification and straining cannot heal. As the Psalm says: Unless Yahweh builds the house, they labor in vain who build it (Psalm 127:1).

And yet God demands genuine cooperation. What could possibly manifest a human will except freely willed labor?

The image given is an iron tool — an axe, a saw. The tool does the actual striking and cutting. It becomes worn and exhausted in the work; the wear is real. But there is a master worker who holds it, puts it in motion, and guides it. And when the tool is completely spent, it is the Lord working secretly within who consoles the heart and renews the strength.

We apply the effort; God supplies the grace. We prepare the altar and lay the wood; we beg for the fire we cannot light.

This is the formula the whole book has been built on, arriving now at its deepest form. Grace provides the fire. Faith prepares the altar. Works are the fruit that preserves the walk.

The Garden

What comes of it?

As the light grows in you, the bitter roots begin to change. You find that you no longer have to force yourself toward the good — you begin to want what is life-giving. Not by suppressing the old desire but by acquiring a new one, which is an entirely different mechanism and infinitely more durable.

The homilies compare the transformed soul to a garden of fruit-bearing trees, in which the trees and the leaves themselves are changed into a new nature. The former bitter things are remade into light. The divisions in the mind heal. The soul becomes a unified Temple of Light.

And then the warning, which I pass on exactly as it was given to me: this is not attained by a single act. It comes through labors, many trials, a long period of time, and much striving. You stand patiently at the door of your own heart and wait for it to be opened.

The Witness

by which he has granted to us his precious and exceedingly great promises; that through these you may become partakers of the divine nature.

2 Peter 1:4

But we all, with unveiled face beholding as in a mirror the glory of the Lord, are transformed into the same image from glory to glory, even as from the Lord, the Spirit.

2 Corinthians 3:18

From glory to glory. Stages again — not one leap but a sequence, each brighter than the last. And note the mechanism: we are transformed by beholding. Not by striving at the transformation directly. You become like what you look at.

The promise beneath all of it was made through Ezekiel long before:

I will also give you a new heart, and I will put a new spirit within you; and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you a heart of flesh.

Ezekiel 36:26

A heart of flesh. Not a better stone. A different substance — one that can be moved, that can feel, that can be wounded. The promised outcome of this entire road is not that you become harder. It is that you become softer, which is precisely where the final stage of this book is going.

The Practice: The Iron Tool

Work until you are honestly tired, then stop. Do the labor that is yours — the watchfulness, the daily anchors, the acts of mercy. And when you are spent, stop striving and rest, on the understanding that the master worker continues when the tool is set down.

Behold rather than strain. Once this week, instead of working on a fault, spend the time simply attending to Christ — in the Gospels, in prayer, in silence. Transformation comes by beholding. Test it.

Notice one changed want. Look for one thing you used to force yourself to do that has become easier, or one appetite that has quietly lost some of its grip. Do not manufacture the observation. But if it is there, mark it — that is the garden changing, and it is the only evidence of this stage you are likely to get.

Ask yourself this week: Where am I straining at something only God can do? Where have I stopped working and called it trust?

The Close of the Stage

You have an answer now to the sixth question. How does the heart change?

It changes in hidden places, not on display. It changes in a person who has kept wonder alive and has not let understanding harden into a closed system. It changes slowly, over a lifetime, through thousands of small faithful decisions. And it changes at a level deeper than behavior — by the mingling of God's own life with a human interior, which we cooperate with and cannot accomplish.

What you should be carrying out of this stage is a different set of wants. Not merely better conduct. If the good has begun to appeal to you rather than merely to obligate you, this stage has done its work.

One more stage remains, and it is the summit.

We have asked how to live, how to see, how to remain, how to live together, how the heart is healed, and how it is changed. The last question is the simplest and the largest: what does the transformed person actually become?

The answer is a single word, and everything in this book has been walking toward it.

A Blessing

May you wield the iron tool faithfully, and lay it down when you are spent.

May the bitter roots in you be remade into light. And may you be given, in place of the stone you have been carrying, a heart of flesh.
