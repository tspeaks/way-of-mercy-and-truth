# Chapter Twenty-Five: The Architecture of Growth

Stage Six — Transformation: How does the heart change?

My friend,

The last two chapters may have done something to you that I should now repair.

Having glimpsed what a transformed life looks like — the quiet cedar, the open hand, the heart that no longer has to force itself — you may have become impatient. You may have looked at your own considerable remaining defects and felt a fresh discouragement.

That impatience is itself the problem this chapter treats.

### The Book of Steps

The Liber Graduum, the Book of Steps, came out of a fourth-century Syriac community at some remove from the theological controversies of the West. Their attention stayed relentlessly practical: how does a person actually develop, over a lifetime?

Their central conviction: transformation is not instantaneous. It occurs through distinct, progressive stages, and the process cannot be hurried.

### Two Ways, and How to Read Them

The Book of Steps describes two paths: the Way of the Upright and the Way of the Perfect.

I must handle this carefully, because it is easily misread as a hierarchy — first-class and second-class Christians — and that is not what the author intended and not how I want you to take it.

The Way of the Upright is the life of those who remain fully in the world. They do not flee society. They follow the Lord inside the honorable structures of ordinary life: earning a living, keeping a household, raising children, being loyal to their neighbors. They commit themselves to active charity — feeding the hungry, clothing the naked, welcoming the stranger. The fruits are named plainly: goodness, lowliness, self-control, patience, gentleness, kindness, joy, and peace with everyone.

The Way of the Perfect is total renunciation — no possessions, unceasing prayer, complete withdrawal.

Now hear me on how these relate, because this is where the harm gets done.

The Way of the Upright is your vocation, and it is not a lesser one. It is not the beginner's path or a concession to weakness. It is the path on which the overwhelming majority of faithful people have walked to the summit of this road, and it is the one the entire book you are holding has been preparing you for.

The distinction is not about worth. It is about season and circumstance. A person with children to raise and a household depending on them has obligations that are themselves the material of their formation — not obstacles to it. To abandon those obligations for a "higher" path would not be ascent. It would be desertion with a religious justification, and I have watched people do it and leave real damage behind them.

If a season ever comes — late, after the familial, financial, and relational obligations of a life have been honestly discharged — when someone wishes to devote their remaining energies wholly to prayer, that is a real vocation and I would not discourage it. But it lies far ahead, and it is not better. It is different.

Until then, your calling is to be perfectly Upright: to bring the three pillars into the middle of the world you actually inhabit.

The Patience of Gradual Formation

Here is the lesson the whole chapter exists to deliver.

Modern minds demand instant perfection and immediate victory over every weakness. The ancient teachers knew better. A tree does not produce mature fruit the day it is planted. Character forms slowly, through thousands upon thousands of small faithful decisions, most of which feel like nothing at the time.

And so the question that matters is not am I perfect yet? It is: am I still walking?

That is the whole examination. If the answer is yes, you are doing this correctly, however far the horizon still looks.

### The Witness

Blessed are those whose strength is in you; who have set their hearts on a pilgrimage... They go from strength to strength. — Psalm 84:5, 7

Strength to strength. Not weakness to strength in a single leap — a series of stages, each drawing on the one before.

Let us not be weary in doing good, for we will reap in due season, if we don't give up. — Galatians 6:9

In due season. Not immediately. The harvest has a date and it is not today, and the only failure available to you is giving up before it.

Test this. Scripture describes the righteous life as like the dawning light, that shines more and more until the perfect day (Proverbs 4:18). Nobody watches a sunrise and complains that it is taking too long.

And Christ described the mechanism exactly: a man scatters seed, sleeps and rises night and day, and the seed should spring up and grow, he doesn't know how. For the earth bears fruit: first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear (Mark 4:27–28).

He doesn't know how. The growth is not under the farmer's management. His task is the scattering and the daily rhythm of sleeping and rising.

You do not have to be the full grain today. You only have to be the faithful blade.

### The Practice: The Next Step

Name only the next step. Not the whole distance to the summit. What is the one thing directly in front of you this month? Do that.

Take stock over years, not days. Once, look back five years. You will almost certainly find change you could not perceive while it was occurring. This is the correct timescale for judging yourself, and daily assessment will only ever mislead you.

Refuse the comparison. When you measure yourself against someone further along, remember you are seeing their present and your interior. It is not a comparison; it is a category error.

Ask yourself this week: Am I still walking? What is the single next step, and what have I been doing instead of taking it?

A Blessing

May you lay down your exhausting perfectionism.

May you value the long process of becoming, and not despise the slow small steps of your daily obedience. And may you be content, today, to be the blade.
