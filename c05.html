Stage One — Foundations: How should one live?

My friend,

We come to the end of the first stage, and I want to give you something you can actually carry.

We have covered a great deal of ground. The moral structure of reality. The practical skill of wisdom. The shattering ethic of Jesus. The honest mirror of James. All of it is true and all of it is, I am aware, a great weight to hand someone in five chapters.

So this final chapter is not more teaching. It is a manual — how any of this becomes a life rather than a set of admirable opinions.

For this we turn to a small document called the Didache, the Teaching of the Twelve Apostles: an instruction sheet for people who were trying to figure out how ordinary Christians should get through a Wednesday. Scholars place it anywhere from the middle of the first century to the middle of the second, and I have no standing to settle that; what matters for us is that it is very early, and that it is unmistakably practical.

You may reasonably ask why we need an early church manual when we have the Scriptures. We do not need it to replace anything. We need it because it shows us how the earliest Christians actually practiced what they had received — and because it is more practical about daily habit than almost anything written since.

If the Gospel is the fire, the Didache teaches you how to build the fireplace so that the house does not burn down.

The Two Ways, Again

There are two ways, one of life and one of death, and there is a great difference between the two ways.

We began the stage here and we end it here, which is not repetition but structure. The Way of Life is rooted in a double love — for God, and for your neighbor as yourself. The Way of Death is marked by disorder and by the refusal to recognize another person's dignity.

Everything in this book is, in the end, a commentary on that choice.

Catch It Early

The distinctive genius of the Didache is that it teaches you to intervene early.

It notices that anger is the seed and violence the fruit; that a covetous glance is the seed and betrayal the fruit; that a divided tongue is the seed and a divided life the fruit. And it directs your attention to the seed, because the seed is where intervention is still cheap.

Be not angry, for anger leadeth to murder... be not lustful, for lust leadeth to fornication... be not a liar, since lying leadeth to theft.

This is exactly what James observed:

But each one is tempted, when he is drawn away by his own lust, and enticed. Then the lust, when it has conceived, bears sin; and the sin, when it is full grown, brings forth death.

James 1:14–15

A conception, a pregnancy, a birth. Which means there is a period — often quite a long one — during which the thing can still be met and dealt with at low cost.

Most of us try to intervene at the end, at the visible action, when the whole momentum of the thing is against us. Watch four movements in particular:

Anger — not the flash, which is involuntary, but the second stage, where you begin composing what you will say. That composition is the fruit forming.

Pride — the Didache calls it being "high-flown." The inward drawing-up of the self above someone. It never stays inward. It comes out in tone within the hour.

Desire — the second look, the lingering, the small permission granted to something you know where it leads.

Double-tongued speech — thinking one thing while saying another. A small division that widens.

I want to say something gently here, because this list can be read as a demand for constant vigilance, and constant vigilance is not sustainable and not what is being asked.

You will not catch these every time. You will not catch most of them, at first. What is being built is not perfect interception but noticing — and noticing, at first, will happen entirely in retrospect. You will realize at nine in the evening what began at two in the afternoon. That is not failure. That is the beginning of the skill. The gap between the event and the noticing shortens over years, not weeks.

The Daily Tasks

Virtue stays theoretical until it has a task attached. The Didache gives three, and they are almost embarrassingly concrete:

Share. "Thou shalt not turn away from him that is in want, but shalt share all things with thy brother, and shalt not say that they are thine own." Note the last clause. The demand is not primarily on the wallet; it is on the sense of ownership. To hold your possessions as a steward rather than an owner changes what generosity costs, because you cannot be robbed of what was never quite yours.

Reconcile. Do not let the day end on a broken thing. The instruction is to make peace before you come to prayer. I have found this to be among the most practically transformative habits available to any human being, and one of the hardest, because the whole architecture of an evening conspires against it.

Give without hesitating. When you see a need, respond. The hesitation is where the reasoning enters, and the reasoning is nearly always in favor of not giving.

Do What You Are Able

Now the sentence I most want you to leave this stage carrying. Having laid out demanding standards, the Didache turns and says this:

For if thou art able to bear the whole yoke of the Lord, thou wilt be perfect; but if thou art not able, do what thou art able.

Do what you are able.

I cannot tell you what it was to read that for the first time. I had spent years oscillating between grandiose spiritual ambition and complete collapse, with very little in between, and it had never occurred to me that the tradition itself had anticipated this and made room for it in the second century.

There is no scolding in that sentence. There is no and be ashamed that you cannot do more. It is the voice of someone who has taught many people and knows exactly how this goes.

And it is not a lowering of the standard. The standard stands — thou wilt be perfect is still there. What is offered is a way to keep walking toward a standard you cannot yet meet, without either pretending you have met it or abandoning the road.

The early Christians learned this patience from Christ Himself:

Come to me, all you who labor and are heavily burdened, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart; and you will find rest for your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light.

Matthew 11:28–30

Learn of me. An apprenticeship, in His workshop, at His pace. Not an examination.

The Witness

Return to Psalm 1, where we began, and read it now as a person who has walked five chapters rather than none. The tree, the roots, the water, the fruit in its season.

Read Deuteronomy 30:19 — "I call heaven and earth to witness against you this day, that I have set before you life and death, the blessing and the curse: therefore choose life, that you may live, you and your seed."

And note that the Didache instructs believers to pray the Lord's Prayer three times daily. Not once, with great feeling. Three times, as a rhythm — because the ancient teachers understood something that took me years: continuity matters more

than intensity. The mediocre practice sustained for thirty years will form you. The magnificent practice sustained for three weeks will not.

The Practice: The Rule of Return

This is the practice that governs all the others, and it is the one I would give you if I could give you only one.

The rule: When you break a discipline, return the same day.

Not tomorrow. Not Monday. Not the first of the month. Not after a suitable interval of feeling bad about it. The same day, at the next available moment, with no ceremony and no self-recrimination.

Why this matters more than anything else in this book: Almost nobody abandons this road through a decision. They abandon it through the accumulation of postponed returns. Four missed days become a fortnight; a fortnight becomes the settled conviction that one is simply not the sort of person who sustains such things.

The break was never the problem. The gap after it was.

How it looks: You forgot the morning anchor. It is now three in the afternoon. You do not restart tomorrow. You stop where you are, for thirty seconds, and say: Today I choose the Way of Life. Then you carry on with your afternoon. That is the whole thing. It is nearly frictionless, and it is the difference between a practice that lasts a lifetime and one that lasts a month.

Ask yourself: How long did I wait, the last time I fell? What would returning have looked like an hour later?

The Close of the Stage

You now have an answer to the question that governs this stage. How should one live?

Live as though reality has a shape, because it does. Acquire the practical skill of wisdom, slowly, by being teachable. Take mercy as your first response rather than your last resort. Audit yourself honestly and treat others generously, never the reverse. Build small daily habits and return to them quickly when you fall.

That is the foundation. It is not glamorous. It is what everything else in this book will stand on.

In the next stage the question changes, and the change is significant. We stop asking how to live and begin asking how to see — because there is a great deal in front of you that you are not yet able to perceive, and some of it is beautiful, and some of it is deception, and you will need to be able to tell.

But not yet. Stay here a while. There is no prize for hurrying.

A Blessing

May your habits become quiet and steady, and may they hold you on the days when your feelings do not. May you do what you are able, and be at peace with the fact that it is not yet everything. And when you fall — today, tomorrow, and in twenty years — may you find that the road is still exactly where you left it, and that no one has moved on without you.

Go now, and walk gently. There is a great deal of time.
