# Jonah — review package

Scripture: World English Bible (public domain). Index status: complete — all 48 verses; reviewed. Method version 0.4.

Every entry below shows the passage in full, then the claim made about it. Read the passage before the claim. The question is not whether the tags are internally consistent — it is whether they survive contact with the text.

**Note.** First indexed narrative book. Two method changes were forced by the genre. (1) A 'mode' field distinguishes a passage that prescribes a practice, one that exemplifies it, and one that shows its absence — Jonah teaches largely by counter-example, and a tag that could not say so would misrepresent the text. (2) The stricter practice test from the James review is applied throughout: a practice is tagged only where the passage prescribes or enacts it, never where it merely shares its subject matter.

**Genre note.** Narrative. Pericopes are scenes rather than arguments, and the protagonist is a negative exemplar for most of the book. Entries tagged counter-example are still real anchors: the curriculum uses failure as instruction throughout, and Jonah is the canon's most sustained portrait of a man who holds correct doctrine and refuses the mercy that follows from it.

**Method rule.** A counter-example entry carries the practice tag whose absence the passage demonstrates. The mode field exists so that a negative anchor can be recorded honestly; leaving practices empty on counter-examples defeats it and hides real anchors.

## 1. Jonah 1:1-3 — The call, and the flight to Tarshish

> **1:1** Now the word of Yahweh came to Jonah the son of Amittai, saying,
> **1:2** “Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and preach against it, for their wickedness has come up before me.”
> **1:3** But Jonah rose up to flee to Tarshish from the presence of Yahweh. He went down to Joppa, and found a ship going to Tarshish; so he paid its fare, and went down into it, to go with them to Tarshish from the presence of Yahweh.

**Claim.** The Two Ways presented as a single decision made in one direction. Nothing is argued and nothing is denied — Jonah simply goes the other way, which is how the Way of Death is described in C01: not dramatic apostasy but a movement no single day ever contains.

## 2. Jonah 1:4-6 — The storm, and the sleeper below deck

> **1:4** But Yahweh sent out a great wind on the sea, and there was a mighty storm on the sea, so that the ship was likely to break up.
> **1:5** Then the mariners were afraid, and every man cried to his god. They threw the cargo that was in the ship into the sea to lighten the ship. But Jonah had gone down into the innermost parts of the ship, and he was laying down, and was fast asleep.
> **1:6** So the shipmaster came to him, and said to him, “What do you mean, sleeper? Arise, call on your God! Maybe your God will notice us, so that we won’t perish.”

**Claim.** The one man aboard who knows the cause of the storm is asleep in the hold while everyone else prays. Watchfulness is defined here by its absence, and it takes a pagan captain to issue the summons.

**Tags.** mode: counter-example (the passage shows what its absence costs) · pillars: humility · stages: S5 · chapters: C20 · practices: PR12 · confidence: high

**Amended at review** — PR12 added at review. The entry already argued that Watchfulness is defined here by its absence, then recorded no tag for it — an inconsistency in the drafter's own method, since Jonah 4:1-3 carried PR15 on exactly that reasoning.

## 3. Jonah 1:7-10 — The lot falls; Jonah confesses the God he is fleeing

> **1:7** They all said to each other, “Come, let us cast lots, that we may know who is responsible for this evil that is on us.” So they cast lots, and the lot fell on Jonah.
> **1:8** Then they asked him, “Tell us, please, for whose cause this evil is on us. What is your occupation? Where do you come from? What is your country? Of what people are you?”
> **1:9** He said to them, “I am a Hebrew, and I fear Yahweh, the God of heaven, who has made the sea and the dry land.”
> **1:10** Then were the men exceedingly afraid, and said to him, “What is this that you have done?” For the men knew that he was fleeing from the presence of Yahweh, because he had told them.

**Claim.** He states his theology accurately — the God who made the sea and the dry land — while in flight across that same sea. The most compact demonstration in Scripture of the claim C12 makes: that assent is not the evidence, and confession without corresponding movement proves nothing.

**Tags.** mode: counter-example (the passage shows what its absence costs) · pillars: truth · stages: S3 · chapters: C12 · practices: none · confidence: high

## 4. Jonah 1:11-16 — The sailors row hard before they throw him over

> **1:11** Then they said to him, “What shall we do to you, that the sea may be calm to us?” For the sea grew more and more stormy.
> **1:12** He said to them, “Take me up, and throw me into the sea. Then the sea will be calm for you; for I know that because of me this great storm is on you.”
> **1:13** Nevertheless the men rowed hard to get them back to the land; but they could not, for the sea grew more and more stormy against them.
> **1:14** Therefore they cried to Yahweh, and said, “We beg you, Yahweh, we beg you, don’t let us die for this man’s life, and don’t lay on us innocent blood; for you, Yahweh, have done as it pleased you.”
> **1:15** So they took up Jonah, and threw him into the sea; and the sea ceased its raging.
> **1:16** Then the men feared Yahweh exceedingly; and they offered a sacrifice to Yahweh, and made vows.

**Claim.** The pagan crew tries to save the man whose disobedience is drowning them, and grieves before acting. Mercy is shown by the outsiders while the prophet supplies only a correct diagnosis, which reverses the assumed direction of moral competence.

**Tags.** mode: exemplifies (someone in the passage does it) · pillars: mercy · stages: S4 · chapters: C16, C17 · practices: none · confidence: high

## 5. Jonah 1:17 — The great fish

> **1:17** Yahweh prepared a great fish to swallow up Jonah, and Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights.

**Claim.** The instrument of rescue arrives looking exactly like the instrument of death. C19's claim that the way home is a door rather than a wall, given in the least reassuring form available.

**Tags.** mode: exemplifies (someone in the passage does it) · pillars: mercy · stages: S5 · chapters: C19 · practices: none · confidence: medium

## 6. Jonah 2:1-6 — The prayer out of the fish's belly

> **2:1** Then Jonah prayed to Yahweh, his God, out of the fish’s belly.
> **2:2** He said, “I called because of my affliction to Yahweh. He answered me. Out of the belly of Sheol I cried. You heard my voice.
> **2:3** For you threw me into the depths, in the heart of the seas. The flood was all around me. All your waves and your billows passed over me.
> **2:4** I said, ‘I have been banished from your sight; yet I will look again toward your holy temple.’
> **2:5** The waters surrounded me, even to the soul. The deep was around me. The weeds were wrapped around my head.
> **2:6** I went down to the bottoms of the mountains. The earth barred me in forever: yet have you brought up my life from the pit, Yahweh my God.

**Claim.** Lament offered from inside the consequence, without any request that the consequence be lifted. It names the depths, the waters, and the weeds in full, which is the honesty Sacred Lament requires and the reason the practice is not the same as complaint.

**Tags.** mode: exemplifies (someone in the passage does it) · pillars: truth, humility · stages: S3, S5 · chapters: C09, C19 · practices: PR08 · confidence: high

## 7. Jonah 2:7 — When my soul fainted, I remembered Yahweh

> **2:7** “When my soul fainted within me, I remembered Yahweh. My prayer came in to you, into your holy temple.

**Claim.** Remembrance arrives at the point of collapse rather than before it. The Failure Recovery Protocol turns on exactly this: that the return is available after the fall, and is often not available earlier.

**Tags.** mode: exemplifies (someone in the passage does it) · pillars: humility · stages: S5 · chapters: C19 · practices: PR11 · confidence: high

## 8. Jonah 2:8-10 — Those who regard lying vanities forsake their own mercy

> **2:8** Those who regard lying vanities forsake their own mercy.
> **2:9** But I will sacrifice to you with the voice of thanksgiving. I will pay that which I have vowed. Salvation belongs to Yahweh.”
> **2:10** Yahweh spoke to the fish, and it vomited out Jonah on the dry land.

**Claim.** Attachment to what is empty is described as forfeiting a mercy already held out. Holy Detachment argued as loss rather than as discipline, which is the more accurate and less flattering form of the argument.

## 9. Jonah 3:1-3 — The word came to Jonah the second time

> **3:1** The word of Yahweh came to Jonah the second time, saying,
> **3:2** “Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and preach to it the message that I give you.”
> **3:3** So Jonah arose, and went to Nineveh, according to the word of Yahweh. Now Nineveh was an exceedingly great city, three days’ journey across.

**Claim.** The identical commission, reissued without alteration, reproach, or probation. This is the message of the second chance that C19 draws from Hermas, and it stands here in the Hebrew Scriptures with no conditions attached to it.

**Tags.** mode: exemplifies (someone in the passage does it) · pillars: mercy · stages: S5 · chapters: C19 · practices: PR11 · confidence: high

## 10. Jonah 3:4-5 — Eight words of preaching, and a city believes

> **3:4** Jonah began to enter into the city a day’s journey, and he cried out, and said, “In forty days, Nineveh will be overthrown!”
> **3:5** The people of Nineveh believed God; and they proclaimed a fast, and put on sackcloth, from their greatest even to their least.

**Claim.** The shortest recorded sermon produces the largest recorded repentance, delivered by a man who wants it to fail. The fruit test of C08 applied to the message rather than to the messenger, and they come apart completely.

**Tags.** mode: exemplifies (someone in the passage does it) · pillars: truth · stages: S2 · chapters: C08 · practices: none · confidence: high

## 11. Jonah 3:6-9 — The king leaves the throne for ashes

> **3:6** The news reached the king of Nineveh, and he arose from his throne, and took off his royal robe, covered himself with sackcloth, and sat in ashes.
> **3:7** He made a proclamation and published through Nineveh by the decree of the king and his nobles, saying, “Let neither man nor animal, herd nor flock, taste anything; let them not feed, nor drink water;
> **3:8** but let them be covered with sackcloth, both man and animal, and let them cry mightily to God. Yes, let them turn everyone from his evil way, and from the violence that is in his hands.
> **3:9** Who knows whether God will not turn and relent, and turn away from his fierce anger, so that we might not perish?”

**Claim.** The man with the most status to lose descends first and furthest, and the city follows from greatest to least. The decree names the evil way and the violence in their hands outright, which is corporate self-accusation, and clothes it in fasting and ashes.

**Tags.** mode: exemplifies (someone in the passage does it) · pillars: humility · stages: S4 · chapters: C16 · practices: PR03, PR08 · confidence: high

**Amended at review** — Questioned on audit and retained. PR08 is grief and confusion expressed honestly before God, while Nineveh's sackcloth is penitential. The tag stands; the tension is recorded here rather than resolved by deletion, so a later reader can weigh it against the passage.

## 12. Jonah 3:10 — God relented of the disaster

> **3:10** God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way. God relented of the disaster which he said he would do to them, and he didn’t do it.

**Claim.** What God saw was their works, not their sackcloth. Merciful Reintegration performed by God toward a foreign empire, which sets the ceiling for how far the practice is meant to reach.

**Tags.** mode: exemplifies (someone in the passage does it) · pillars: mercy · stages: S1, S5 · chapters: C03, C19 · practices: PR29 · confidence: high

## 13. Jonah 4:1-3 — Jonah is angry that God is merciful

> **4:1** But it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was angry.
> **4:2** He prayed to Yahweh, and said, “Please, Yahweh, wasn’t this what I said when I was still in my own country? Therefore I hurried to flee to Tarshish, for I knew that you are a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness, and you relent of doing harm.
> **4:3** Therefore now, Yahweh, take, I beg you, my life from me; for it is better for me to die than to live.”

**Claim.** He recites the mercy formula of Exodus 34:6 correctly and offers it as his grievance — he fled because he knew God would forgive. Harshness exposed as a doctrinally literate position, which is why Shedding Harshness is placed as late in the road as it is.

**Tags.** mode: counter-example (the passage shows what its absence costs) · pillars: mercy, humility · stages: S7 · chapters: C27 · practices: PR15 · confidence: high

## 14. Jonah 4:4 — Is it right for you to be angry?

> **4:4** Yahweh said, “Is it right for you to be angry?”

**Claim.** God conducts the Moral Audit by asking a single question and then waiting. No accusation is made and no verdict is given; the man is simply required to look. The practice in its purest form, administered from outside.

**Tags.** mode: prescribes (the passage commands this practice) · pillars: truth · stages: S1 · chapters: C04 · practices: PR01 · confidence: high

## 15. Jonah 4:5-8 — The booth, the vine, and the worm

> **4:5** Then Jonah went out of the city, and sat on the east side of the city, and there made himself a booth, and sat under it in the shade, until he might see what would become of the city.
> **4:6** Yahweh God prepared a vine, and made it to come up over Jonah, that it might be a shade over his head, to deliver him from his discomfort. So Jonah was exceedingly glad because of the vine.
> **4:7** But God prepared a worm at dawn the next day, and it chewed on the vine, so that it withered.
> **4:8** It happened, when the sun arose, that God prepared a sultry east wind; and the sun beat on Jonah’s head, so that he fainted, and requested for himself that he might die, and said, “It is better for me to die than to live.”

**Claim.** A man who felt nothing for a city grieves violently over a plant that shaded his head for a day. Disordered attachment made visible by scale, which is the diagnostic Holy Detachment depends on.

**Tags.** mode: counter-example (the passage shows what its absence costs) · pillars: humility · stages: S4, S6 · chapters: C18 · practices: none · confidence: high

## 16. Jonah 4:9-11 — Should I not be concerned for that great city?

> **4:9** God said to Jonah, “Is it right for you to be angry about the vine?” He said, “I am right to be angry, even to death.”
> **4:10** Yahweh said, “You have been concerned for the vine, for which you have not labored, neither made it grow; which came up in a night, and perished in a night.
> **4:11** Shouldn’t I be concerned for Nineveh, that great city, in which are more than one hundred twenty thousand persons who can’t discern between their right hand and their left hand; and also much livestock?”

**Claim.** The book ends on a question about the reach of concern and records no answer, leaving the reader to supply one. The widening circle of C27 argued by arithmetic — one vine against a hundred and twenty thousand people who cannot tell their right hand from their left.

**Tags.** mode: prescribes (the passage commands this practice) · pillars: mercy · stages: S7 · chapters: C27, C29 · practices: PR15 · confidence: high
