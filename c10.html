Chapter Ten: The Delight of Remaining

Stage Three — Abiding: How should one remain?

My friend,

I want to talk about joy, and I want to explain first why it belongs in a book about moral formation, because to a certain kind of serious person it will seem like an ornament.

It is not an ornament. It is load-bearing.

Here is what I learned the hard way. A faith sustained by duty alone has a lifespan. It can be quite long — I have known people who ran on grim obligation for decades — but it ends, and it usually ends in one of two ways: a collapse that surprises everyone, or a slow hardening in which the person becomes technically righteous and impossible to be near.

Duty is a fine servant and a terrible engine.

The Odes and the Voice from Inside

For this we turn to the Odes of Solomon, a collection of early Christian hymns.

I wondered for a long time why we needed these when we already have the Psalms. The answer took years to appreciate: the Odes speak from a particular position that is rare in religious writing. Most spiritual literature is written by someone pursuing God. The Odes are written from inside the relationship — the voice of a lover who has already found the beloved and is simply speaking from there.

They are formative poetry. They are not designed to instruct your behavior; they are designed to work on your affections, which is a slower and more permanent business. Their language is saturated with gratitude, wonder, communion, beauty.

And their argument, if a song can be said to have one, is this: life in the Way is not a burden of duty but a response of trust to a love that got there first.

From Duty to Delight

Four things ripen as you learn to remain in that love. I list them not as targets but as symptoms, so that you can recognize them when they appear:

Trust. You lean into God's presence without bracing.

Participation. You stop observing the light from outside it and start moving around inside it. The truth is no longer a proposition you assent to at a distance — it is a place you are standing.

Receptivity. You learn the strange skill of simply receiving, which is much harder for capable people than giving. Some of us would rather do almost anything than be given something.

Communion. The wall between you and God turns out to have been a door, and you notice you are not alone in the room, and possibly have not been for some time.

The Witness

I have set Yahweh always before me. Because he is at my right hand, I shall not be moved. Therefore my heart is glad, and my tongue rejoices. My body shall also dwell in safety... You will show me the path of life. In your presence is fullness of joy. In your right hand there are pleasures forevermore. — Psalm 16:8–11

Follow the logic of that Psalm, because it is precise. Because He is at my right hand — therefore my heart is glad. The gladness is not manufactured by David. It is a consequence of a location.

Which is the whole point. You cannot decide to be joyful. You can only stay near the source of it and let the thing happen.

Test this. This is exactly what Christ promised, and He said it in the same breath as the command to remain: I have spoken these things to you, that my joy may remain in

you, and that your joy may be made full (John 15:11). Note whose joy it is. My joy — His, transferred, not yours, generated.

A Word If You Feel Nothing

I need to say something here, because this chapter could wound someone, and I would rather risk the digression.

Some of you will read all this about delight and gratitude and communion and feel precisely nothing. Perhaps you have felt nothing for a long time. Perhaps you are doing everything in this book faithfully and the interior weather has been grey for two years and this chapter reads like an account of a country you have not been permitted to enter.

I want to tell you three things.

Your faithfulness is not invalidated by your dryness. The person who keeps remaining while feeling nothing is doing something harder and more admirable than the person swept along on warm feeling. If you are still here, still practicing, still returning — that is not a lesser form of this. In some traditions it is considered the more advanced one.

Feeling is not the fruit. Read Psalm 16 again. The promised outcome is I shall not be moved — stability, not sensation. Joy in Scripture is much closer to a settled orientation than to an emotional state, and the two can go long periods without coinciding.

And do not diagnose yourself too quickly. Persistent flatness sometimes has ordinary causes — exhaustion, grief, illness of body or mind — and treating a medical or circumstantial matter as a spiritual failure adds shame to a burden that is already heavy enough. If the greyness is deep or long, tell someone who can actually help. That is not a detour from this road. It is walking it sensibly.

The Practice: The Discipline of Gratitude

Three things, named specifically, daily. Not a general sense of thankfulness — three actual items. Specificity is what makes this work; vagueness is what makes it useless. Do it whether or not you feel grateful. The feeling is not the point and may arrive months later.

Read something that sings. Once a week, read the Psalms or the Odes not for instruction but the way you would listen to music. Do not analyse. Let it work on you.

Notice one beautiful thing and stop. Actually stop. Ten seconds. Most of us register beauty in passing and never let it land.

Ask yourself this week: When did I last enjoy God rather than merely serve Him? What am I grateful for that I have never bothered to say?

A Blessing

May you lay down the religion you have been carrying and find that something was carrying you the whole time.

May praise become as natural to you as breathing. And if it does not — may you keep breathing anyway, and know that the breathing counts.
